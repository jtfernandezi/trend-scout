# Trend Scout — Report 2026-09-12

**Run type:** scheduled research pass (thirty-ninth run under the venture-scale-gated thesis).
**Outcome:** **2 surviving ideas (composites 4.3 and 4.0, one brief), 14 logged kills, 2 held-book
entries updated with new evidence.**

The 2026-09-10 report left three instructions and all three were followed.

**One — invert Steps 3 and 4; run occupancy before the wedge is written.** Done without exception.
Every candidate this run opened with a `[segment] + [workflow] + startup` search before any of the four
parts were filled in, and it worked exactly as predicted: eight of the fourteen kills died on the first
search, before an hour was spent arguing a wedge that was never going to matter. The two survivors are
the two candidates that came back empty.

**Two — add the platform-owner question to the config-change test.** Done, and it is now the second
most productive test in the book. Three kills came from it directly (agency-side procurement, PLC code,
right-of-way records) and one more effectively (foundry methoding, where MAGMA owns the simulation
record). The question — *who owns the record this reviews, and have they shipped an agent against it?*
— is answerable in one search and it retired four candidates that all looked good on paper.

**Three — start from a misfit, not a round.** Attempted honestly and it half worked, which is worth
recording precisely. Complaint-mining (G2, Reddit, forums) produced almost nothing usable through
search: the results are SEO content about AI vendor red flags, not practitioners saying "we tried it,
it doesn't fit us." **Pricing-page mining did work** — Harvey's floor (about $1,200 per seat per month
with a ~25-seat minimum, roughly $360k/year to start, sold demo-first) is published, precise, and
immediately identifies who is excluded — but the segment it identifies was, of course, already
occupied by six companies, because a published price floor is a misfit everybody can read. **The
geography axis is the one that paid.** The ledger's 364 prior entries contain the word "sovereign"
zero times, "Germany" zero times, "Brazil" zero times, "data residency" zero times and "Japan" once.
One of this run's two survivors sits in the UK for a structural reason, and its largest expansion leg
is a Dutch statutory event nobody in this book has ever looked at.

**The kill distribution, and the first change in it for six runs.** Eight of fourteen kills came from
the occupancy test, four from the platform-owner form of the config-change test, one from the plain
config-change test and one was a duplicate of a prior ledger entry. Occupancy is still dominant but it
is down from eleven-of-seventeen and ten-of-eleven in the two runs before that, and the reason is
methodological rather than environmental: running occupancy first means the candidates that survive it
are then killed by something else, instead of everything being killed by occupancy at the end. **The
config-change test is not weak; it was being asked too late.**

**The finding worth carrying forward.** Both survivors have the same shape, and it is the shape this
book keeps rediscovering: *reconstruct an entitlement from primary evidence that nobody has read in
forty years, and sell the determination with the liability attached to whoever pays when it is wrong.*
What is new is the second half — in both cases the party who should own the record is **legally or
commercially barred from being the one who audits it**, and in both cases the existing occupants are
hourly-billing services firms whose published technology stack is a website. That combination —
conflicted incumbent, unfunded human occupant, frontier-model-shaped task, and a counterparty who
already prices the uncertainty as a cash loading — is the most reliable survivor signature in
thirty-nine runs.

## Surviving ideas

### 1. Coverage archaeology as a machine — find the 1960s insurance policy that pays for the PFAS settlement

**In plain English.** When a company gets sued today over pollution it caused decades ago — or a
church, school or youth charity gets sued over abuse from the 1970s — the money to pay the settlement
usually has to come from insurance policies bought in the 1960s and 70s, because insurers stopped
covering this kind of thing in 1986. Those old policies are almost always real, and the paperwork
proving they existed is almost always lost: it's in warehouse boxes, microfilm, old board minutes and
handwritten payment ledgers, at insurance companies that changed their names four times since. Today a
tiny handful of firms find them by paying researchers to read boxes by hand at hundreds of dollars an
hour. We do it with a machine that reads all the paper at once, works out which long-dead insurer is
which company today, rebuilds what the missing policies said from the standard forms of that year, and
hands over a court-ready chart of exactly what coverage existed — and we take a share of the money
recovered instead of a fee, so it costs the customer nothing to find out.

- **The proven pattern.** Harvey — $550M at $15.5B on 2026-09-09, revenue above $400M, 80% of the top
  100 US law firms — proves frontier models do senior-professional document judgement over a huge
  private historical corpus. Forus ($150M at $3B, free to doctors and patients) proves the payment
  model: whoever profits when the transaction completes funds the paperwork.
- **Who buys the narrowed version.** The GC or risk officer at a company just tendered a legacy claim
  (a CERCLA PRP letter, a PFAS suit at a manufacturer, plating shop, airport, fire-training site or
  paper mill); and the restructuring officer of a diocese, school system or municipality inside an
  open abuse lookback window, whose reorganisation plan is funded by historical insurance and nothing
  else. Trigger: the tender, with a claims bar date on it.
- **Why the leader can't follow.** Four parties, four separate blocks. The occupants — PolicyFind,
  Restorical Research, Arcina Risk Group, Insurance Archaeology Group — are hourly research boutiques
  whose published technology stacks are WordPress, PHP and Google Fonts, with no funding, no
  engineering and no incentive to delete their own billable unit. The brokers (Marsh, Aon, Gallagher)
  are the parties whose record-keeping failure created the gap, so systematically machine-reading
  their own archives manufactures discoverable evidence against themselves. The carriers are the
  adverse party by construction. Harvey sells seats to firms that bill hourly and cannot take a
  contingent position in someone else's insurance asset.
- **Expansion path.** Contingency reconstruction for PFAS and abuse defendants → a standing
  coverage-asset register sold into M&A diligence, successor-liability disputes and environmental
  reserving → financing and then *buying* the coverage claim outright, which puts you on the
  policyholder side of a pool estimated at **$120–165bn for PFAS alone**, opposite the multi-billion
  legacy-liability acquirers (Enstar, RiverStone, Marco) who built their businesses on the carrier
  side of the same asset.
- **Scores.** Wedge 5 · Pattern 4 · Market 4 · Defensibility 5 · YC-fit 4 · Founder-fit 4 →
  **composite 4.3**
- **Biggest risk.** The beachhead's *services* market is small — insurance archaeology is a few
  boutiques, not a category — so the $1B case rests entirely on moving from fees to owning or
  financing the recovered asset, which is a capital business with a longer cash cycle than a software
  company and will need a founder comfortable underwriting outcomes.

### 2. Benefit of record — prove what a pension scheme actually owes, so the buyout can close

**In plain English.** A company pension scheme that promised people a pension for life eventually pays
an insurance company to take the whole thing over, and then it can shut down. Before that can happen,
somebody has to establish exactly what every single member is owed — and the rules are scattered
across forty years of legal documents, amendments, letters and meeting minutes written by different
lawyers who often contradicted each other. Today nobody really knows, so the insurer adds a large
margin to the price to cover the risk that the numbers are wrong, and the whole transaction drags for
years. We read all of it with a machine, work out what each member is genuinely entitled to and why,
and stand behind the answer — so the insurer can drop its risk margin and the scheme can close in
months instead of years. The insurer and the pension trustees split the bill, and it is far less than
the margin it removes.

- **The proven pattern.** Harvey again — the same mechanism, re-pointed a second way. The market
  evidence around the buyer is its own signal: **~300 UK schemes reaching buyout in 2026 against
  £40bn+ of deal volume**, after ~160 buy-in-to-buyout conversions in 2025 (**+30% YoY**), and XPS's
  Q3 2026 trustee update reporting that schemes are taking *longer* to get to buyout with insurer
  backlogs growing — cause named as data and governance.
- **Who buys the narrowed version.** The pricing actuary at a bulk-annuity insurer (Standard Life,
  L&G, Rothesay, PIC, M&G, Aviva, Just) and the endgame lead on the trustee board — the two parties
  who split the data-risk loading. Trigger: a buy-in has completed and must convert to buyout.
  Secondary: the **31 October 2026 pensions dashboards connection deadline** for occupational schemes
  with 100+ members that have not fully bought out, and sponsors seeking to release surplus under the
  Pensions Regulator's 2026 flexibilities.
- **Why the leader can't follow.** Everyone who could is the party being checked. The administrators
  (Capita, Equiniti, Aptia, Trafalgar House) wrote the calculation code and made the payments; an
  honest machine audit is an itemised inventory of their own historical errors, and the rectification
  work is billed by the firm that caused it. The consultancies (WTW, Aon, Mercer, Hymans, Barnett
  Waddingham) authored the benefit specifications and the data cleanse being audited — drafter, not
  auditor — and the manual project fee is the revenue being deleted. Harvey has no basis to carry a
  calculation liability. Independence is a property of who you are *not*, and it does not ship in a
  release note.
- **Expansion path.** UK DB buyout (£40bn/yr of premium; 5,000 schemes and £1.2tn still to transact) →
  run-on and surplus release → become the administrator once benefits are machine-readable → **the
  Netherlands, where the Wet toekomst pensioenen forces conversion of ~€1.5tn of accrued entitlement
  into individual pots before 2028** — the largest forced benefit-reconstruction event in the world →
  US pension risk transfer (~$40–50bn/yr, already carrying annuity-provider-selection litigation).
- **Scores.** Wedge 4 · Pattern 4 · Market 4 · Defensibility 4 · YC-fit 4 · Founder-fit 4 →
  **composite 4.0**
- **Biggest risk.** The buying centre is trustee boards and insurance pricing committees, which is
  about as slow as institutional selling gets; and "pension data" is a phrase the conflicted
  incumbents already own commercially, so the first sale is as much about proving independence as
  proving accuracy.

## Held book

**ts-0031 — independent behavioural-certification lab for robot foundation-model policies** (status
`watching`, unchanged) — updated, and this is the first update that *narrows* the idea. **Robocurve
(YC Summer 2026, Demo Day 2026-09-10)** is building real-world evaluations of physical AI: open-source
tooling and independent, reproducible benchmarks scoring how well robot models do real jobs, on the
explicit argument that labs evaluate in-house, nobody runs continuous benchmarking as a service, and
sim-strong models degrade on contact with the world. That is this entry's founding observation, said
by someone else, with YC behind it. What it is *not*: Robocurve is a Public Benefit Corporation
publishing open benchmarks, and a PBC whose legitimacy rests on openness structurally cannot also sell
the confidential, per-deployment, liability-bearing certificate an underwriter conditions cover on —
the two products poison each other. So the seat this entry claims (paid behavioural assurance sold to
the insurer, re-run on every OTA policy update) is still empty, **but any version of this idea that
leads with "independent benchmarks" rather than "insurable evidence" is now dead.** Conditions kept
improving around it: humanoids took $8.6B by mid-2026 (1.8x all of 2025); Agility's Digit is past
65,000 operating hours across nine customer facilities; and the widely-cited 14-month payback
arithmetic explicitly assumes 80% uptime — an assurance question wearing an operations costume.

**ts-0054 — correlated-AI-failure catastrophe model and per-insured AI-exposure score for primary P&C
carriers** (status `watching`, unchanged) — updated with one supportive datapoint and one that sharpens
the buyer. Supportive: **AI startups took 95.2% of all insurtech funding in Q1 2026** on $1.63B of
total insurtech investment, and the industry's own commentary now states flatly that policies are
silent, ambiguous or exclude autonomous software, that cyber and E&O are excluding AI claims, and that
**traditional insurers lack the data and technical capability to assess deployed AI systems** — which
is this entry's thesis restated by the market. Sharpening: **Risklytics (YC Summer 2026)** is selling
insurance for frontier-technology businesses including robotics and data centres — a *primary writer*
entering the risk, the third such entrant logged in five weeks after Goodfault and Corgi. The
asymmetry is the point: capital keeps arriving on the primary-writing side and never on the
accumulation side, which is why the seat is open — and also a warning, because if primary writers keep
multiplying, the accumulation model gets *acquired by a reinsurer* rather than sold to fifty carriers.
That changes the venture-scale story and should be re-tested next time this entry is touched.

## Method note for the next run

**One. Keep occupancy-first; it changed the kill distribution in one run.** Occupancy fell from
eleven-of-seventeen to eight-of-fourteen, not because the world emptied out but because candidates
that clear it now get killed by the *right* test. The platform-owner question is the second most
productive test in the book on its first full outing — four kills — and both should now be treated as
fixed steps, asked in that order, before any of the four parts are written.

**Two. Stop re-pointing mechanisms that come with a hardware channel.** CloudNC and 9 Mothers between
them produced five kills and no survivors, and the reason is the same in both cases: in metalworking
and in defence airspace, the software is sold through, or bundled with, machines and effectors that
have incumbent dealer networks and years of installed base. A funding announcement makes these look
like young categories. They are old categories with a new interface. Prefer mechanisms whose only
artefact is a document or a determination — both survivors are of that kind, and so were the survivors
of 09-03, 09-05 and 09-08.

**Three. The next run should mine the geography axis properly, because it was the only new axis that
paid.** This run reached it almost by accident, through a UK statutory calendar, and immediately found
empty ground in a market with £1.2tn behind it and a €1.5tn forced conversion next door. The ledger has
*zero* prior entries mentioning sovereignty, data residency, Germany or Brazil, and one mentioning
Japan. Two specific, dated, non-US events are sitting unexamined and should be worked directly: the
**Dutch Wet toekomst pensioenen conversion deadline of 1 January 2028**, and the **EU Cyber Resilience
Act**, whose reporting obligations became applicable on **11 September 2026 — yesterday** — with the
main manufacturer obligations following in December 2027. (The CRA was glanced at this run and set
aside because ONEKEY, Exein and Cybellum occupy the manufacturer-side product; the *distributor and
importer* obligations under Articles 19-20 were not properly tested and may not be occupied.) A dated
obligation in a jurisdiction where the US leaders have no legal standing is the closest thing this book
has found to structurally defensible ground.
