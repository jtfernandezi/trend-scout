**Outcome:** **2 surviving ideas (composites 4.2 and 3.8, one brief), 10 logged kills, one held-book

The 2026-09-19 report left three instructions and all three were followed.

**One — search occupancy before writing the four parts.** Done without exception, and the saving was
the run. Six of this run's ten kills died on the first or second search, before a single one of the
four parts was written. The two survivors are, once again, the two candidates whose occupancy searches
came back empty.

**Two — go back to the barriered side and pick the barrier first.** Done literally. The run opened on
the three barriers the last report named as unworked — state-licensed insurance adjusting and
appraisal, accredited laboratory certification outside the killed testing-network frame, and
court-appointed roles other than the fiduciary one killed at ts-0405 — and then on a fourth the search
turned up on its own: **delegated public authority**, where the government hands a private organisation
the power to issue the approval itself. Two of the three named barriers produced kills. The fourth
produced a survivor.

**Three — treat the YC Fall 2026 RFS as an exclusion zone.** No candidate this run landed inside one
of the thirteen squares, so the filter was not exercised. It stands.

**The finding worth carrying forward, and it retires a whole class of candidate.** ts-0430 (an
AI-native Chapter 7 estate administration firm) was killed on the venture-scale gate before occupancy
was even searched, and the arithmetic generalises past this one idea. **A court-appointed or
statutorily compensated role cannot become a billion-dollar company, because the appointing authority
rather than the market sets the price.** The Chapter 7 no-asset trustee fee was doubled by Act of
Congress on 2026-02-06 — from 60 dollars to 120, the first increase since 1994 — which is as loud a
why-now as this book ever gets, and it sizes the entire national pool at a few hundred million dollars
against roughly 370,000 annual filings. The same ceiling killed ts-0405 (guardianship) in September.
Court-appointed roles are now a **closed barrier** for this ledger; the licence is real, the
compensation is capped by the body that grants it, and no amount of machine leverage moves a fee
schedule written into statute. Do not open another.

**The second finding, which is the corrective to the first.** The barrier that *does* survive is the
one where the government delegates not a role but an **authority** — the power to issue the approval
itself, and to keep the resulting certificate as property. The FAA's Organization Designation
Authorization is the clearest example in US law: the holder issues supplemental type certificates on
the FAA's behalf, and an STC is an asset that earns a royalty on every subsequent installation. That is
the difference between being paid a court-set fee for an hour of fiduciary work and owning a
royalty-bearing library. It is the whole of the gap between ts-0430 and ts-0424.

**The kill distribution.** Four of ten on the occupancy test, four on the config-change test (two of
those in the strongest possible form — the platform owner had already shipped it), one on the
venture-scale gate, one on the AI-native gate plus occupancy. Occupancy leads for the eighth
consecutive run, but only just: this is the first run in several where the config-change test matched
it, and the reason is worth naming. Three of the four config-change kills died because **the leverage
the idea depended on is now a product anyone can buy** — Verisk's XactAI inside Xactimate, Facility
Grid's autonomous commissioning agent, Nephron's and Omnicell's compounding robots. In 2026 an
automation advantage that ships as somebody's feature is not a wedge.

## Three mechanism-level conclusions

**(a) Court-appointed roles are a closed barrier, and the reason is arithmetic rather than
competition.** ts-0430 joins ts-0405 and ts-0406. The pattern is that a court or a statute sets the
compensation, the appointment is discretionary and non-transferable, and the revenue therefore cannot
compound no matter how much machine leverage is applied. The 2026-09-19 method note listed
"court-appointed roles other than the fiduciary and guardianship one" as an unworked barrier; it has
now been worked, and it should be struck from the list rather than re-opened. **A licence that a
software company cannot hold is only valuable when the market, not the licensor, sets the price.**

**(b) In 2026, an automation advantage that ships as somebody's feature is not a wedge — and this now
kills as many candidates as occupancy does.** Three of this run's four config-change kills follow the
identical shape: the idea's whole claim was "we do this with machines and the incumbent does it with
people," and the machines turned out to be available to the incumbent as a product, from the platform
owner, this quarter. Verisk put generative AI inside Xactimate, which every property adjuster in North
America already licenses. Facility Grid put an autonomous agent inside the commissioning software the
largest commissioning providers already run. Nephron and Omnicell have had compounding robots for
years. **The question to ask of every candidate from now on is not "can the incumbent automate this?"
but "can the incumbent buy the automation from its existing vendor?"** — and in a mature software
category the answer is usually yes. The wedge has to be something the incumbent cannot purchase:
a statutory conflict, a liability posture, a cost structure, an authority.

**(c) Delegated authority is a better barrier than a licence, because the output is property.** This is
the run's genuinely new finding and it is the reason ts-0424 survives. A licence lets you do the work
and bill for it; the ceiling is the hour. A **delegated authority** — the FAA's Organization
Designation Authorization is the cleanest US example — lets you *issue the approval*, and the approval
is an asset. A supplemental type certificate is property that earns a royalty on every subsequent
installation, so the same machine leverage that would merely compress a consultancy's billable hours
instead accumulates a portfolio. The barrier blocks the two obvious follower classes cleanly: a tool
vendor cannot sign, and an OEM's own authority is scoped to its own products. The next run should look
for the other places the US government delegates approval authority to private organisations and ask
the same question of each.

### 1. The fiduciary that small-town America cannot afford — an AI-native municipal advisor

**In plain English.** When a school district, a small town or a rural water system needs to borrow
money — to replace a roof, a treatment plant, a fire station — it is supposed to have an independent
financial advisor on its side of the table, someone whose legal duty is to the town rather than to the
bank lending the money. Most small places do not have one, because the fee on a four-million-dollar
loan does not pay for a banker's week, so they simply take whatever terms the bank proposes. We
register as that advisor and do the work with machines instead: read the town's audited accounts, its
existing loans and their fine print, its budget and its rate studies, and tell it what it can actually
afford and how the deal should be structured — then keep the paperwork right for the next twenty years.
The town pays a fee it can afford for advice it has never had; there are roughly fifty thousand of
these borrowers in America and about five hundred billion dollars of this borrowing every year.

**The proven pattern.** Corridor, 25M dollar seed on 2026-09-21 led by Bain Capital Ventures, built
explicitly on the fact that small accounts pay less commission while taking the same amount of a
licensed advisor's time — inside a six-company cluster (AGI at ~70M dollars committed and >50 percent
agency profitability improvement across a 10-agency pilot; Beacon at 225M dollars and one acquisition a
week; Long Lake at 100M dollars of EBITDA in two years; Current at >300M dollars of revenue; Integral)
all doing the same job.

**Who buys the narrowed version.** The finance director, business manager or superintendent of a
district, authority or small city issuing 2-25M dollars, at the moment a bond election passes, a state
revolving-fund or USDA loan offer lands, a call date arrives, or a rate study produces a capital number
the existing debt service cannot carry.

**Why the leader can't follow.** Three different blocks for three classes of follower. The underwriters
and placement banks are *statutorily* barred — under the SEC Municipal Advisor Rule a broker-dealer
underwriting the issue owes the issuer no fiduciary duty and cannot be its municipal advisor on it, and
serving this segment honestly often means telling the issuer to refuse the bank's loan. The established
advisory firms (PFM, Hilltop, Baker Tilly, Stifel) have a cost structure of senior bankers and a fee
tied to par, which a 3M dollar issue cannot carry. And the issuer-software vendors (BondLink, on the
2026 GovTech 100 with >100bn dollars of client par; DebtBook, which shipped AI analyst features this
year) would have to assume a statutory fiduciary duty and — per the SEC's Office of Municipal
Securities statement of **2026-09-16** — direct primary and secondary antifraud liability under Section
17(a) and Section 10(b) for disclosure they help prepare, *regardless of contractual disclaimers*. That
is a different liability posture and it puts them in competition with their own channel.

**Expansion path.** Per-deal advisory → the recurring layer the same relationship owns (continuing
disclosure under Rule 15c2-12, material event notices, arbitrage rebate, covenant monitoring), which
turns a lumpy fee into annual revenue per issuer → origination, aggregating hundreds of small issues
into pooled rated programmes and earning placement economics on flow. Underneath it accumulates a
continuously maintained, machine-readable financial and disclosure record for every small issuer in
America — separately valuable to the buy side of a 4 trillion dollar market whose disclosure is
famously late and incomparable.

**Scores.** Wedge 4 · Pattern 5 · Market 4 · Defensibility 4 · YC-fit 4 · Founder-fit 4 → **composite
4.2**

**Biggest risk.** Fiduciary liability is the product and also the exposure: the SEC just said in
writing that an advisor who touches the official statement owns antifraud liability for it, and a
machine-drafted disclosure that misses a material fact in a live bond deal is an existential event, not
a bug report. The mitigation and the moat are the same thing — the inquiry has to be more exhaustive
than a human's, not faster than a human's.

### 2. Own the stamp, not the stamping tool — an AI-native FAA certification house

**In plain English.** Before anyone can legally change an aircraft — new avionics, a cargo conversion,
a new interior, a defence sensor, a drone variant — the government has to approve the change, and the
approval is a mountain of engineering paperwork proving the modification is safe. The government does
not do all this itself: it hands certain private organisations the authority to issue the approval on
its behalf. Today those organisations are engineering consultancies that charge by the hour, so the
paperwork is the product and slowness is the business model. We become one of them and produce that
paperwork by machine, with the licensed engineers reviewing and signing. And here is the part that
makes it a company rather than a consultancy: the approval certificate is **property**. Every aircraft
that is later modified under it pays us. So instead of billing hours, we accumulate a library of
approvals that earns money while we sleep.

**The proven pattern.** The same AI-native licensed-firm cluster (Beacon, AGI, Integral, Corridor),
pointed at a delegated public authority rather than a private licence. Demand evidence: the GAO found
that of **23 electric aircraft type certification projects begun with the FAA since 2018, only 6 have
been approved**; flight-test and certification engineers are named among the scarcest roles in US
aerospace; and the FAA has been expanding the ODA programme on industry input.

**Who buys the narrowed version.** The programme or engineering lead at a modification shop, avionics
or systems supplier, cargo converter, defence integrator or uncrewed-aircraft manufacturer whose
product cannot be sold until an approval exists — at the moment a customer purchase order or fleet
commitment is conditioned on a certificate that does not yet exist, or a programme has already slipped
once on certification.

**Why the leader can't follow.** A tool vendor cannot sign. Ansys SCADE, LDRA, Parasoft, Vector and
Visure hold the qualified toolchains and sell to applicants; they cannot hold the delegation, cannot
issue the certificate and cannot take the liability, and their customers are exactly the people this
disintermediates — the newly funded tooling entrant (Xcert AI) sits on the same side of that line. An
OEM's own authority is scoped to its own products and cannot be pointed at third parties. And the
incumbent certification consultancies bill per engineering hour, so automating the campaign deletes the
revenue that funds it.

**Expansion path.** Fixed-price modification approvals with a schedule commitment → a certificate
portfolio that earns an installation royalty independent of new campaigns → EASA and bilateral
validations, parts manufacturer approvals, defence airworthiness, and the incoming uncrewed and
advanced-air-mobility approval wave.

**Scores.** Wedge 3 · Pattern 4 · Market 4 · Defensibility 4 · YC-fit 4 · Founder-fit 4 → **composite
3.8**

**Biggest risk.** The wedge is rated 3 honestly: nothing stops a well-capitalised AI roll-up from
simply **buying** an ODA holder, and Beacon closes an acquisition roughly every week. The delegation
transfers with FAA approval; it is friction, not a wall. The defensible asset is the certificate
library and the requirement-to-evidence corpus, not the authority itself — which means the race is to
accumulate approvals fast, and losing that race is how this idea dies.

## Method note for the next run

**One. Add the purchasability question to the config-change test, and ask it second.** After
"could the leader serve this segment next quarter?", ask **"can the incumbent buy this automation from
a vendor it already pays?"** Three of this run's four config-change kills died on that question alone
and would have survived the original one. In a mature software category the platform owner ships the
agent into the workflow the incumbent already licenses, and the entrant's entire advantage evaporates
on a renewal. This is cheap to ask — it is one search of the incumbent's existing vendor's changelog —
and it should run immediately after the occupancy search.

**Two. Mine delegated authority as its own barrier class, and strike court appointment from the list.**
Conclusion (c) is the run's new tool: look for the places a government delegates the *power to issue an
approval* to a private organisation, because the output is property rather than a fee. Beyond the FAA's
ODA, candidates worth opening on include FDA accredited-persons third-party review under 510(k), USDA
process authorities, PHMSA special permits, and the accredited conformity-assessment bodies under
emerging EU product regimes. Against each, ask the ts-0430 test first: **who sets the price — the
market, or the body that granted the authority?** If it is the granting body, kill it before writing
the four parts.

**Three. The third named barrier is still unworked, and it should be opened next.** Of the three
barriers the 2026-09-19 note listed, insurance adjusting was killed (ts-0428, by the platform owner),
court appointment was killed as a class, and **accredited laboratory certification outside the killed
testing-network frame was only half-tested** — the EUDR attempt (ts-0431) died because the regime
turned out not to require certification at all, which says nothing about regimes that do. The specific
question to open with: which accreditation regimes make the accredited body **legally unable to
consult** on what it certifies, so that the consultancies are structurally barred from following, and
of those, which are attached to a market the market rather than the accreditor prices.
