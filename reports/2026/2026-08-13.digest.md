**The survivor — force-controlled robots on the meat plant cutting floor.**
In a meat plant the worst job is breaking down carcasses: deboning hams, taking chicken breasts off the bone, trimming fat. Every animal is a slightly different shape, so machines have never managed it — it still takes sixty to eighty people standing in a cold, wet room with knives, and plants can't fill those jobs any more, both because the workforce is being deported and because three-quarters of the people quit within a year anyway. This is a company that puts the new by-feel robots on that line and charges the plant a few cents per animal — less than the wages it replaces, and it gets more usable meat off each bone than a tired person on hour nine, which is worth more to the plant than the labour saving is.

Why now: US plants ran last year at 87% of the staff they needed, deportations are projected above 1.5 million this year, and automated deboning was still a laboratory result as of this month. The trick is charging per animal rather than selling machines — sold as machines it's a small business inside an existing equipment maker's category; sold as labour, the pool it's eating is $20–25 billion a year in the US alone.

**From the kills:** three of the eight died to companies that shipped or raised in the last six months — a hospital surgical-instrument robot, a laundry-handling robot with a $14m round, and Google's robotics arm launching in July the exact "robots without a specialist to install them" product I'd worked out. Robotics squares are closing in weeks now, not years. New rule added: in physical products, check who owns the platform before checking who owns the customer — the platform owner gives away any layer that sells more robots.
