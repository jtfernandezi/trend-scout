**The survivor — the as-built disposition engine.**
Aeroplane factories find small problems constantly: a hole drilled a fraction off, a scratch slightly too deep, a bracket a hair out of place. Someone senior has to decide whether the part is still safe to fly — and that takes days, is done by engineers who are retiring in droves, and while it waits the unfinished job travels down the line with the half-built plane. This is software that makes that call in minutes and writes out the proof for an engineer to sign.

Why now, and why it's worth a lot: in March Boeing said its permission to build planes faster is no longer a fixed number — it's now tied to exactly that measure of unfinished, travelling work. Boeing and Airbus have seventeen thousand planes on order they can't deliver for eleven years, so speeding up the line is worth billions. Nobody sells the thing that clears that queue: the existing software files the paperwork, and the actual decision is made by expensive consultants who bill by the hour.

That's now a check to run first. And Europe just pushed its big AI law deadline out to December 2027, which quietly kills off a whole family of "help companies comply" ideas this project has already rejected four times.
