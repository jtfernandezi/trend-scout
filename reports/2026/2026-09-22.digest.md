*Trend Scout — 2026-09-22*

*2 survivors, 10 kills.*

*1. The fiduciary small-town America can't afford (composite 4.2)*
When a school district or a small town borrows money to replace a roof or a water plant, it is supposed
to have an independent advisor on its side of the table — someone whose legal duty is to the town, not
to the bank lending the money. Most small places don't have one, because the fee on a four-million
-dollar loan doesn't pay for a banker's week, so they just take whatever the bank offers. We register
as that advisor and do the work with machines: read the town's accounts, its existing loans and their
fine print, its budget and rate studies, tell it what it can actually afford and how the deal should be
structured — then keep the paperwork right for the next twenty years.
*For:* the finance director or superintendent of a district, water authority or small city borrowing
2-25 million dollars, the moment a bond election passes or a loan offer lands. There are ~50,000 of
these borrowers and ~500bn dollars of this borrowing a year, in a 4 trillion dollar market.

*2. Own the stamp, not the stamping tool (composite 3.8)*
Before anyone can legally change an aircraft — new avionics, a cargo conversion, a defence sensor, a
drone variant — the government must approve it, and the approval is a mountain of engineering paperwork
proving the change is safe. The government doesn't do all this itself: it hands certain private firms
the authority to issue the approval on its behalf. Today those firms are consultancies that charge by
the hour, so slowness is the business model. We become one of them and produce the paperwork by
machine, with licensed engineers reviewing and signing. The part that makes it a company: the approval
certificate is *property*, and every aircraft later modified under it pays us a royalty.
*For:* the programme lead at a modification shop, avionics supplier, cargo converter, defence
integrator or drone maker whose product can't be sold until a certificate exists that doesn't yet.

*Also worth knowing:* this run closed a whole category. An AI-native bankruptcy trustee firm looked
strong — Congress doubled the trustee's fee in February, the first raise since 1994, precisely because
nobody can afford to look for assets — and it died on arithmetic, not competition. When a court or a
statute sets your price, no amount of automation makes you a big company. That kills court-appointed
roles as a class for this book, and it points at the thing that *does* work: authorities where the
government lets you issue the approval and keep the certificate, because then the output is property
rather than a fee. That's why the aviation idea survived.

The other new lesson is sharper and more uncomfortable: in 2026, automating something faster than the
incumbent isn't a wedge if the incumbent can *buy* the automation from a vendor it already pays. Three
of this run's kills died exactly that way — the catastrophe-claims idea to Verisk shipping AI inside
Xactimate, which every adjuster in North America already licenses; a data-centre commissioning idea to
the commissioning software having already shipped an autonomous agent; a robot pharmacy idea because
the incumbents already own the robots.
