*1. The machine coroner (composite 4.0)*
When someone dies unexpectedly — an overdose, a crash, a possible homicide, an old person found at
home — the county is legally required to work out what killed them, and only a specially trained doctor
called a forensic pathologist can make that call. America has about 850 of them and needs about 1,250,
and the rules of their own profession cap each one at 250 autopsies a year, so the shortage cannot be
fixed by working harder: families now wait more than a hundred days for an answer and murder trials sit
waiting for a report. We become the company a county hires to do this work — a machine pulls together
everything the health system already knows about the person who died, which today is one investigator
on the phone for a week; a body scanner does the job of the scalpel in the many cases where it can; and
the scarce doctor spends their hours on the judgement and the signature instead of the paperwork.
*For:* the county board and the coroner or medical examiner who hold the death-investigation contract,
at the moment their one pathologist retires or a prosecutor puts the backlog on the record. About
605,000 cases a year at roughly $3,000 of budget each is $1.8bn of public spend, and not one company in
it is a technology company.

*Also worth knowing:* the most useful result this run was a correction to our own reasoning. This book
keeps arguing that an incumbent "can't automate itself because it sells people's time." That turned out
to be only half true. A water company paid a fixed fee per plant automates itself gladly — Veolia has
already opened an AI command centre to do exactly that, which killed one candidate outright. The wedge
only works when the incumbent is paid *per procedure*, so that doing the job more cheaply destroys its
own revenue. The surviving idea passes that test with the incumbent's own price list: $1,250 for an
autopsy, $600 for the scanner-led alternative.

Eleven of the fourteen kills were the same story as ever — someone funded had already taken the segment,
usually found on the first search. Notable: Japan's mandatory inheritance-registration backlog (taken,
three deep), the German statutory audit (taken by a Berlin startup partnered with the largest mid-market
audit firm), and 401(k) plan audits (taken, with a pilot cohort running this autumn).
