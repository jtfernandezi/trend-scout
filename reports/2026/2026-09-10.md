# Trend Scout — Report 2026-09-10

**Run type:** scheduled research pass (thirty-eighth run under the venture-scale-gated thesis).
**Outcome:** **zero surviving ideas, 17 logged kills, 2 held-book entries updated with new evidence.**
No brief was written, because nothing earned one.

This is the second zero-survivor run in the book (2026-09-01 was the first, zero-for-nine). It is a
larger and more deliberate zero than that one: seventeen candidates were developed to a full four-part
specification against six freshly-scanned mechanisms, and every one died. Reporting it honestly is
worth more than forcing a seventeenth-best idea into a card.

**The 2026-09-08 report left three instructions and all three were followed.** Open on a mechanism
outside the human-inquiry family — done, none of this run's six source mechanisms touch interviewing.
Prefer triggers behind professional standards and state licensure over contested federal rulemakings —
done, and the one statutory trigger used (EUDAMED's mandatory modules, live 2026-05-28) sits behind an
EU regulation with no opposing litigant. Test the two unmined mechanisms it named — **self-pay
diagnostics was mined hard** (Scan.com produced four of this run's candidates) and **the
deployment-labour constraint was examined and deliberately not developed**, for reasons given below.

**The kill distribution.** Eleven of seventeen kills came from the occupancy test, four from the
config-change test, one from the venture-scale gate, one from segment viability, and one was a
duplicate of the operator's own held book. That is the fifth consecutive run in which occupancy is the
dominant killer, and in nine of the eleven the occupant was funded and shipping the identical product.

**The one genuinely new lesson, and it is a correction to how this book has been reasoning.** Four
candidates this run had a *textbook* structural block on the leader — manufacturer money is legally
barred from funding off-label access (ts-0364); a charity must be independent of its donors
(ts-0355); a software vendor will not assume EU Article 11 product liability (ts-0358); an agent
vendor cannot supervise its own speech (ts-0362). Every one of them still died, because someone had
already occupied the segment. **A structural block on the leader does not imply an empty segment.**
The block explains why the leader is absent; it says nothing about who walked in while they were.
Steps 3 and 4 have been run in the wrong order — the wedge was argued first and occupancy tested
after. Next run should invert it and spend the first search of every candidate on occupancy, before
the wedge is written down at all.

## Breakout startups scanned

1. **Forus** — **$150M Series C at a $3B valuation on 2026-09-08**, led by Bain Capital Ventures with
   every existing institutional investor reinvesting (Thrive, General Catalyst, Accel, Redpoint,
   BoxGroup, Pear, Vast, SV Angel). The round **tripled the valuation months after the Series B** and
   took equity past $300M. Traction: thousands of medical practices and health systems in **all 50
   states**, **9 of the 15 largest biopharma companies**, approval times cut from several weeks to
   **under 48 hours**, and the product is **free to both doctors and patients**. **Mechanism:** the
   party who profits when a transaction completes pays for an agent network that clears the
   administrative gauntlet blocking it, so the workflow is free to the people doing the paperwork —
   which buys distribution no software vendor could purchase.

2. **Scan.com** — **$220M** combined equity and debt closed August 2026 ($90M equity led by Noteus
   Partners; $130M debt from VerisFi and Atempo), after **doubling revenue past a $165M annualised run
   rate** and serving **900,000+ patients** across the US and UK. Context: the US runs ~600M scans a
   year and **85% are still booked by fax or phone**. **Mechanism:** a model reads a referral,
   understands which capacity actually satisfies it — live scanner availability, subspecialty, price —
   and routes it in real time across thousands of independent owner-operators, turning fragmented idle
   licensed capacity into one national network sold as an API.

3. **Corgi** — regulatory approval to operate a **full-stack insurance carrier**, and **ARR past $40M
   in the 14 months since approval in July 2025**, on **$108M** raised (Y Combinator, Kindred,
   Contrary, Glade Brook, Seven Stars, SV Angel and others). Not a broker, not an MGA riding someone
   else's paper: it owns underwriting, balance sheet and claims. **Mechanism:** frontier models collapse
   the operating cost of a regulated risk-bearing entity far enough that a de novo carrier can
   profitably serve a segment whose premiums are too small for an incumbent's expense ratio.

4. **Blee** — **$20M Series A on 2026-09-08** co-led by Fin Capital and SMBC Fin Atlas Beyond

   customers include **SoFi and Expedia Group**, review times cut **by as much as 65%**.

   model must adjudicate content against a rulebook before it publishes and keep monitoring it after.

5. **AIR, with Zenity and Obsidian** (category cluster) — AIR came out of stealth **2026-09-01 with

6. **Split Pay** — **$125M** across a combined Series A and B (Khosla leading both; Thrive Capital and

**Three mechanisms are now exhausted and should not be re-mined.**

**The Forus family (five candidates, five kills).** The manufacturer-funded, free-to-the-constrained-party
access network is a beautiful mechanism and every commercially obvious re-point of it is taken. Devices,
trial sites, value analysis and off-label all died to named funded occupants; the building-products
version died to the approval body itself. The residual insight worth keeping is the *legal boundary*:
manufacturer money cannot reach a federal beneficiary's cost-sharing and cannot fund off-label access
at all. That boundary is durable and will still be true in a year — it is simply not, by itself, an
opening.

**The Scan.com family (four candidates, four kills).** Machine-routed capacity networks fail in a
consistent and predictable way, and the pattern is worth stating so no future run rediscovers it:
**routing is only the constraint when the capacity is a commodity and the buyer is the one who pays.**
Imaging satisfies both. Infusion does not (the drug must be acquired and the payer contract held).
Theranostics does not (the dose decays and the manufacturer already runs the network). Veterinary does
not (the consolidator owns the capacity). Test labs do not (the majors own the chambers). Before
developing another capacity-routing candidate, ask those two questions first and expect to stop.

**The Blee family (two candidates, two kills), for a reason that generalises past this mechanism.**
Both died to the *system-of-record owner shipping the agent first* — Veeva in pharma promotional
review, the agent vendors themselves in conversational supervision. This is the ts-0178 platform-owner
rule again: when the review gate lives inside somebody's system of record, that somebody ships the
reviewer. Pre-publication adjudication is only winnable where no vendor owns the record.

**Two mechanisms were examined and deliberately not developed, which is also a result.** *Split Pay*
had the scan's most extreme traction (70x in a year, ~1M sign-ups) and no AI-native re-point: its
product is timing and pricing, and every narrowing tested — commercial obligations, Medicaid-funded
providers, small landlords — either failed the AI-native gate outright or landed on established
factoring and BaaS players. *The deployment-labour constraint* (AWS $1B and Microsoft $2.5B into
forward-deployed engineering against ~95% of enterprise AI pilots delivering no measurable impact) was
named by the last report as untested "as a product." It was examined and left alone: the product
version is discovering and formalising a customer's undocumented process from its own exhaust, and the
ledger has already killed that twice (ts-0319, ts-0320) with Celonis holding the category above them.
Recording this so the instruction is not reissued a third time.

## Surviving ideas

**None.** No brief was written this run.

Two candidates came closest and it is worth naming where they stopped, since neither died to the
occupancy test that killed most of the field. **ts-0355** (the AI-native independent patient-assistance
foundation) had the best wedge of the entire run — donor independence is a statutory condition, so no
manufacturer-funded competitor can ever occupy the seat — and died on the venture-scale gate because
the entity that holds the wedge cannot have owners. **ts-0349** (building-product code approval) was
the only candidate where no credible occupant was found, and died because the leader in the narrowed
market turned out to be the approval body itself, which is entrenched rather than blocked. Both are
clean gate failures rather than close calls, and neither should be revived.

## Held book

**ts-0031 — independent behavioural-certification lab for robot foundation-model policies** (status
`watching`, unchanged) — updated, and the update is that **the buyer changed**. Through 2026 the
assumed customer was the robot maker or the deploying enterprise. The insurance market has now moved
in front of both: Goodfault underwrites humanoids and software agents on one engine, "priced on what
the machine is permitted to do, with credits for safeguards enforced in code," and the trade press
states plainly that ISO 10218 and ISO 3691-4 are "the difference between a risk an underwriter can
price and one they have to decline." An underwriter is a better customer than a robot maker: it pays
because the evidence lowers its own loss cost, it renews annually, and it can make the certificate a
condition of cover. The standards picture tightened the same way — **ISO 10218-1:2025 and 10218-2:2025
replaced the 2011 editions and absorbed ISO/TS 15066**, but the mobile-robot and humanoid provisions
are not expected until **2027-2028**, leaving a two-year window in which learned policies operate near
people with no applicable standard. Capital context: physical AI took **$47.4B across 521 deals in H1
2026**, roughly 4x H2 2025; robotics startups have raised **$18.8B in 2026 against $15B in all of
2025**. Still no occupant. This is the third consecutive run in which the conditions improved and the
seat stayed empty.

**ts-0054 — correlated-AI-failure catastrophe model and per-insured AI-exposure score for primary P&C
carriers** (status `watching`, unchanged) — updated, because the accumulation it prices stopped being
hypothetical. **AIR reports filtering out about 27% of the agent add-ons and skills it finds online**,
with strongest demand from financial services and pharma. A quarter of a *shared, externally sourced*
capability supply being unsafe is exactly the shape of a correlated exposure: many insureds pulling the
same component from the same public registry. It is now measured rather than argued. Around it, $435M
went into 12 agent security and governance financings between April and September 2026, and Goodfault
has begun writing the primary risk — which means carriers will need a view on aggregation across their
books whether or not they write it deliberately. No occupant found for the accumulation model itself.

## Method note for the next run

**One. Invert Steps 3 and 4. Run occupancy before the wedge is written.** This run's central finding
is that four candidates with textbook structural blocks on the leader still died to occupancy, because
the block explains the leader's absence and says nothing about who walked in. The 2026-08-29 run
adopted occupancy-first on the two-sentence version and it worked; this run drifted back to arguing
the wedge first on the strongest-looking candidates and paid for it four times. Make it a rule with no
exception: **first search of every candidate is `[segment] + [workflow] + startup`, before any of the
four parts are filled in.**

**Two. Add the platform-owner question to the config-change test explicitly.** Three kills this run
(ts-0356, ts-0357, ts-0362) were the same failure: the party that owns the system of record or the
runtime shipped the agent itself. Veeva owns the promotional record, Microsoft owns the agent runtime,
the agent vendors own their own transcripts. The question to ask before developing any
review-adjudicate-or-certify candidate is **"who owns the record this reviews, and have they shipped
an agent against it?"** — one search on the leader's changelog answers it. The ledger already calls
this the ts-0178 platform-owner rule; it should be a checklist item, not a remembered precedent.

**Three. The next run needs a different kind of source, not a different mechanism.** Six mechanisms
were scanned and every one of them was a funded US B2B startup found through funding coverage. That
sourcing produces mechanisms whose obvious re-points have already been taken by the people reading the
same funding coverage — which is a decent explanation for five consecutive occupancy-dominated runs.
The watchlist names three source types this run did not use at all: **reviews and complaints** (G2,
Reddit, industry forums — who says "we tried it, it doesn't fit us"), **the leaders' own pricing pages
and careers pages** (what the floor price excludes, which markets they are not staffing), and
**non-US markets**. One search against a funding announcement finds a mechanism everyone else has;
one search against a complaint thread finds a buyer nobody has served. Next run should start from a
misfit, not from a round.
