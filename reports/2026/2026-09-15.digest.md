*1. The AI-native jail doctor (composite 4.2)*
Every county jail has to provide healthcare to the people it holds, and nearly all of them hire an
outside company to do it — but the three biggest of those companies went bankrupt in the last two
years under lawsuits over people who died in custody, and counties are firing them and looking for
someone else. At the same time the rules changed so a jail can now bill Medicaid for care given in the
ninety days before someone's release, which is money none of the incumbents' systems can even produce
a bill for. We become that outside company, built around AI: when someone is booked in at 3am and
can't say what medication they're on, the system finds out from every record the county already has;
the nurse talks and the notes and the bill write themselves; and the system predicts who is actually
getting released soon, which is the thing that decides whether the new money can be claimed at all.
*For:* the county sheriff and board of supervisors putting a jail medical contract out to tender —
typically after an in-custody death — in one of the 19 states where the new Medicaid benefit is live.
US correctional health spend is around $9bn; a mid-sized county contract is $25m over three years.

*2. The machine company doctor (composite 4.0)*
In the Netherlands, if an employee goes off sick the employer must keep paying their wages for two
full years and follow a strict legal procedure, with the first deadline at six weeks, to get them back
to work — and if the paperwork isn't good enough the government can make them pay for a third year.
The procedure has to be run by a specially registered company doctor, and the country is running out
of them: it needs at least 258 new ones a year and only about 90 start, so the wait for an appointment
has stretched to four or five weeks against a six-week legal deadline. We become the occupational
health service employers hire, with a machine doing the interview, the assessment and the
deadline-chasing and the scarce doctor reviewing and signing — which Dutch rules already allow.
*For:* the HR director at a Dutch company with 50–1,000 staff, at the moment an employee passes six
weeks off sick. Every absent employee costs them €250–400 a day, so every day earlier pays for itself.

*Also worth knowing:* the most useful result this run was a negative one. The EU Data Act — which from
three days ago forces manufacturers to hand over the data their machines generate — looked like a
perfect opening, and four different versions of it died, all for the same reason: the people who
needed that data had already worked around the lock years ago with add-on sensors and third-party
trackers. A law that unlocks data is only an opportunity if the workaround was impossible. Europe's
regulatory calendar is now largely picked over; what is still empty is licensed professions whose
supply is provably shrinking against a workload the law fixes.
