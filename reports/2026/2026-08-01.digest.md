**Trend Scout — 2026-08-01**

**1 survivor · 11 kills — second run in a row to break the drought, and the biggest kill count yet.**

I led with the two freshest capabilities on the board — the July 28 result where a model found a novel attack on a post-quantum cipher in 60 hours that two years of expert human review had missed, and every frontier lab converging on multi-hour agents in a single month — and crossed them into eleven industries. Ten died, nine of them to a named funded company found on the first search. The one that survived came from the run's most under-discussed signal: what the AI capex boom is doing to the *rest* of the electronics economy.

**The survivor — ts-0141: the firmware re-hosting engine.** *(composite 4.3 — second-highest of 152 ledger entries)*

Almost everything with a plug or an engine — a car's dashboard, a factory controller, an infusion pump, a lift controller — runs on a small memory chip somebody chose fifteen years ago. Those chips are now disappearing, because the factories that made them have been switched to making memory for AI data centres, which this year swallow about **70% of the world's supply** (it was 20-30% in 2022). When a chip is discontinued, the manufacturer has to redesign the product, rewrite the software, and then *prove* to a regulator or a carmaker's change board that the new version behaves exactly like the old one. That costs **$20,000 to $2 million every time**, takes a year, and is done by hand by contract engineers who are already fully booked. This is a machine that does it: hand it the old software and the replacement chip, and it rewrites the code, runs it side by side against the original on real hardware until it can show the behaviour is identical, and produces the paperwork proving it. Manufacturers pay happily, because the alternative is a production line that stops.

**Why it's still open — and this is now a pattern, not a coincidence.** Two runs in a row the survivor came through the same door: *the people best placed to build it are the people whose revenue it deletes.* Here that's the contract firmware shops who bill for exactly this work. And the parties who hold the data are worse than slow — they're **opposed**: the obsolescence-alert vendors (Z2Data, SiliconExpert) sell notification and stop where the engineering starts, the toolchain vendors sell per-chip licences, and the chip makers' entire business model is lock-in, so a tool that makes silicon interchangeable is hostile to them.

**Why now, to the week.** Q2 2026 memory contract prices rose a record 90-95% in a single quarter, legacy DDR2/DDR3 (what cars and factories run on) is being starved as fabs convert to AI memory, no new capacity arrives until late 2027, and SK Hynix says it may last past 2030. Meanwhile the agent capability to do verified multi-day engineering work landed in July.

**Biggest risk:** safety-critical firmware is the least forgiving place to put an autonomous agent. The real product is *proof of equivalence*, not code — expect the first 18 months to go into the hardware test harness, not the AI.

**Killed:** a lab that sells AI cryptanalysis (too few buyers on Earth, and the frontier labs publish it free) · finding chip side-channel bugs before tape-out (Caspia launched the category first) · pricing fixed-price government bids after the April executive order flipped $775B of federal contracting to fixed-price (Deltek owns the data *and* is already running the exact marketing line) · an AI lender for sub-$100k mortgages off the new housing act (the origination stack is crowded and the trigger is a subsidised pilot) · reading an insurer's whole policy wording for hidden exposure (Verisk literally writes the contract language) · AI-written kernels to break NVIDIA's moat (Mako, Luminal) · migrating buildings off the copper phone network (one-time job, hardware vendors own it) · evolving better industrial scheduling algorithms (Google's AlphaEvolve is in production and published) · AI fab scheduling (Flexciton, with Intel) · defending banking apps against AI reverse-engineering (Promon published the benchmark).

**A rule worth keeping:** the freshest, most striking capability of the month generated three ideas and killed all three — the domains where "find the novel weakness" is valuable have very few buyers, and the defenders are sophisticated enough to run it themselves. The company was in the boring second-order consequence of the boom, not in the headline.
