### 1. ts-0032 — Independent test-and-evaluation assurance harness for DoD autonomous systems *(held; re-validated, why-now materially strengthened; new brief)*

**In plain English.** The military is buying drones and weapons that decide things for themselves using AI, and this kind of AI isn't predictable — run the same situation twice and it can behave differently. But before anything reaches real troops a commander has to sign off that it's been tested, and right now there's no good way to produce that evidence. This company is the independent lab that throws millions of nasty, unexpected situations at the AI inside a simulator, finds where it does something dangerous, and hands the government a report stating exactly what it will and won't do. The Pentagon pays because it can't deploy systems it has already bought without this — and it specifically wants the testing done by someone *other than* the company that built the weapon.

**Who buys it.** A DoD program office or T&E authority that must clear a test-and-evaluation gate before a fielding decision on a specific autonomy class — entered via a live DIU/CSO or SBIR solicitation rather than a cold enterprise sale.

**Why no one already serves them.** The primes (Anduril ~$28B, Shield AI ~$5B) grade their own homework and DoD wants independence; FFRDCs like MITRE and APL do it slowly and unproductized; commercial AI-eval vendors are built for chatbots, not embodied contested autonomy. DoD is *funding the framework* and soliciting the harness precisely because no product exists.

**What changed today.** FY2026 gives AI and autonomous systems a **$13.4B standalone budget line — the first ever**; the Pentagon's AI Acceleration Strategy (2026-01-09) mandates an "AI-first" force via seven Pace-Setting Projects; DTE&A has funded researchers to build assurance-case frameworks for autonomous systems and their TEV&V implications; and a DoD SBIR topic "Runtime Assured Autonomy for AI-Driven Unmanned Platforms" is live ($250k Phase I / $2M Phase II).

### 2. ts-0086 — Independent "silicon-delivery realism" engine for the AI buildout's financiers *(held; re-validated, thesis broadened, no builder found)*

**In plain English.** Banks and investors are lending tens of billions of dollars against AI data centres and fleets of AI chips, quietly assuming that because a company *ordered* the chips it will *get* them on time. That assumption is shaky — and worse, everyone's chips come from the same one packaging plant and same three memory makers, so a single hiccup makes hundreds of projects slip simultaneously and the lender who financed all of them eats one giant loss at once. This company reads the public breadcrumbs — earnings calls, customs records, equipment orders, satellite photos of factories — to estimate chip line by chip line whether the silicon will actually arrive, then calculates how badly a lender gets hit if those shared chokepoints break together. Financiers pay for it the way they already pay for hurricane models.

**Who buys it.** The risk or underwriting lead at a GPU-backed-debt lender or neocloud/data-centre asset-backed-securities investor, at the moment they must price collateral that hasn't been delivered yet.

**Why no one already serves them.** Supply-chain mappers (Everstream, Interos) map suppliers generically for procurement, not correlated delivery shortfall for financiers; semiconductor analysts publish commentary, not a per-line model inside an underwriting workflow; the foundries and memory makers own the allocation data but will never sell a truth their own customers use against them.

**What changed today.** CNAS calculates **2nm logic capacity is already triple-overbooked for 2026**, with NVIDIA, AMD and Google having pre-bought virtually every available TSMC wafer while smaller startups queue for **late-2028** slots. And the constraint is now explicitly systemic rather than chip-specific: through Q1 2026 shortages spread into helium, substrates, power components, optics and thermal, with lead times of **20 to 128+ weeks across eight shortage categories** — which widens the dependency graph this engine prices. Still think-tank analysis, not a product.

**Biggest risk.** Allocation data is deliberately opaque and polluted by strategic double-ordering; that is simultaneously the technical core and where the company lives or dies. Plus it is the fourth buildout-chokepoint idea in the book — the same *pattern* as ts-0064/0069.

### 3. ts-0069 — Independent correlated construction-delay / "time-to-power" model for the data-centre buildout *(held; re-validated, thesis quantified, novelty softening)*

**In plain English.** Insurers have started selling cover that pays out when a data centre opens late, and lenders finance them on the promise of an opening date. This company builds the model that maps who depends on which factory, contractor and grid queue, and works out what an insurer's or lender's *whole book* loses when those shared bottlenecks break at once.

**Who buys it.** The exposure-management lead at a delay-in-start-up, builders-risk or parametric carrier (and their reinsurers) writing AI data-centre construction — now that Aon, Descartes and Zurich have all launched capacity into a peril nobody can price.

## Takeaway for next run

**Eight droughts — but this one falsified the comfortable explanation.** The last five reports concluded "the constraint is the method: I scan what everyone scans." So this run changed the input the way those takeaways asked: nine never-mined domains and two never-used frontier capabilities. It still produced zero, and the kill causes were *structurally different* each time. The honest updated conclusion is stronger and less flattering: **the public web does not contain undiscovered points in the (novel × AI-native × $1B) intersection, because any such point is a headline the moment it exists.** Continuing to generate from public sources is not a tuning problem; it is the wrong instrument.

**Two concrete recommendations, in priority order:**

1. **Commit, and commit to two — because the book is narrower than it looks.** The held book is not eight independent bets. Six of eight are the same shape (independent correlated-accumulation model sold to the risk-holder: ts-0048, ts-0054, ts-0064, ts-0069, ts-0086, and the ts-0120 I killed today for being a seventh). Two are a different shape (independent assurance lab: ts-0031, ts-0032, plus ts-0039). **A rational portfolio picks the best of each shape, not the top two by composite.** That means: **ts-0048** (4.3, crisis worsening, verified twice as un-built) as the accumulation-model bet, and **ts-0032** (4.0, but the sharpest dated pull in the book — a first-ever appropriated $13.4B line and a government actively soliciting the missing product) as the assurance-lab bet. ts-0032's brief is now written; it needs a cleared defense-native cofounder more than it needs more research.
2. **Watch two windows that are closing, and stop researching the rest.** ts-0069 and ts-0064 are both being encircled by adjacent players who own the *observation* layer (SynMax; GridUnity) while the accumulation layer stays open — that is a 1-2 quarter window, not a year. ts-0086's novelty is holding best of the accumulation family (still only think-tank analysis, and today's evidence *broadened* the thesis to eight shortage categories). Everything else in the book can be left alone.
3. **If a generative run happens again, it must be seeded from a non-public signal — and this run finally proves why.** Eight scans, 123 ledger entries, six shapes of kill. The next generative attempt should start from something the web cannot see: a conversation with a liquidity/risk lead at a semi-liquid-fund manager (ts-0048), or a T&E authority inside DoD (ts-0032). Both of those conversations also *advance a commit* rather than hunting a 124th idea.
