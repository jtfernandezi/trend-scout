# Trend Scout — Report 2026-09-15

**Run type:** scheduled research pass (fortieth run under the venture-scale-gated thesis).
**Outcome:** **2 surviving ideas (composites 4.2 and 4.0, one brief), 13 logged kills.**

The 2026-09-12 report left three instructions. All three were followed, and the third one — mine the
geography axis properly — is the one that decided the run.

**One — keep occupancy-first.** Done without exception; every candidate opened with a
`[segment] + [workflow] + startup` search before any of the four parts were written. Nine of the
thirteen kills came from it, and in seven of those nine the occupant was found on the first search.
The two survivors are, again, the two candidates that came back empty.

**Two — keep the platform-owner question inside the config-change test.** It fired twice this run
(Pharmacy First, where Accurx already sits inside NHS primary care and the clinical-records phase is
already announced; and the works-council idea, where Personio owns the German employee record). It is
now a cheap, reliable second question and should stay.

**Three — mine the geography axis.** Done, and it produced one survivor and two of the most useful
kills in the book. The instruction was right but the reason it was right has changed: **the European
regulatory calendar is now mostly occupied, and the European *professional licence* is not.** Both of
this run's dated non-US regulatory events — the EU Data Act's access-by-design duty, applicable to
connected products placed on the EU market after **12 September 2026, three days ago**, and the Cyber
Resilience Act's Article 14 reporting obligations, live **11 September 2026, four days ago** — were
worked hard and produced five kills between them and no survivors. The survivor that came out of
Europe came from a statutory *profession* whose supply is collapsing, not from a statutory *deadline*.

**The finding worth carrying forward, and it is the most useful mechanism-level result in several
runs: a regulation that removes a moat is worth nothing if the market already routed around the
moat.** The EU Data Act does exactly what this book looks for — it forces open a dataset that
incumbents held for commercial reasons, on a dated trigger, in a jurisdiction where the US leaders
have no standing. Four separate beachheads were developed against it (lifts, hospital equipment,
mixed-brand extended warranty, lessor residual values) and all four were already occupied — by
companies that solved the data problem years ago with retrofit sensors, third-party telematics and
independent warranty paper, *without* the regulation. The lesson generalises: before treating a
data-access regulation as a why-now, ask what the people who needed that data have been doing for the
last ten years. If they built around it, the regulation confers no advantage on a new entrant; it
just lowers the incumbent occupant's cost.

**The kill distribution.** Nine of thirteen kills from the occupancy test, two from the config-change
test (one in its platform-owner form), one on the venture-scale gate, one on the AI-native gate. The
one venture-scale kill is notable because it is the first in five runs: the German works-council idea
had a genuinely structural conflict and still failed, because the artefact it sells is negotiated once
per deployment and the market around it is a consulting market.

**The shape of both survivors.** Same signature as 09-12, one step further on: *a licensed act whose
supply is a human being, a statutory clock with a priced consequence for missing it, and an incumbent
whose revenue is the hour being automated or whose liability is created by an honest record.* In both
cases the incumbent is not merely slow — it is in bankruptcy court or it is a staffing agency whose
entire moat is the size of its roster of the scarce professional.

**Two mechanism-level conclusions to stop re-mining.** *(a)* **The EU Data Act is not a wedge.** Four
beachheads, four occupancy kills, and in every case the occupant had already solved the data problem

that is still empty is not the calendar — it is the **licensed profession** whose supply is falling
while its statutory workload is fixed. Both of this run's European candidates that survived or nearly
survived came from that, and the one that died in Europe (Steuerberater) died because someone else had
already noticed.

## Surviving ideas

### 1. The AI-native jail doctor — run county correctional healthcare, and bill Medicaid for it

**In plain English.** Every county jail has to provide medical care to the people held in it, and
almost all of them hire an outside company to do it. Those companies are in terrible shape: the three
biggest went bankrupt in the last two years under the weight of lawsuits over people who died in
custody, and counties are firing them and looking for someone else. At the same time a change in the
rules means that, for the first time ever, a jail can bill Medicaid — the government health programme
— for care it gives someone in the ninety days before release, which is money nobody in this business
is set up to collect. We become that outside company, but built around AI: when someone is booked in at
three in the morning and can't tell anyone what medication they're on, our system finds out from every
record the county already has; the nurse talks and the notes and the bill write themselves; and the
system works out who is actually getting released soon, which is the thing that decides whether the
Medicaid money can be claimed at all. The county pays us to run its jail's healthcare, and the money
the incumbent leaves on the table pays for doing it properly.

- **The proven pattern.** The YC Summer 2026 batch's agent-native services cluster — Rational, Billow
  AI and Last Accounting Company in accounting, Osmaura and Erinys in law — plus YC's own request for
  startups saying it outright: sell the service, not the software. The paying proof that the shape
  scales is Savvy Wealth, a 100M dollar Series C at a 600M dollar valuation on 2026-09-09 (up from
  225M in July 2025), 150+ advisers, 19 hours a week saved each.
- **Who buys the narrowed version.** The county sheriff or jail administrator and the board of
  supervisors who hold the jail medical contract. Trigger: that contract coming up for re-tender or
  non-renewal — usually after an in-custody death, a settlement or a state or DOJ finding — in a state
  where the section 1115 reentry benefit has just gone live for that facility.
- **Why the leader can't follow.** Epic and Oracle Health model a patient and an encounter; a jail is
  modelled on custody — housing location, cell moves, segregation, transport, court dates, sick-call
  kites — so serving it is a second product sold through a county procurement motion neither company
  runs. The incumbent contractors are conflicted against their own record: their margin is a fixed
  per-inmate-per-month capitation, and a system that honestly documents every unmet clinical need
  manufactures the evidence for the deliberate-indifference litigation that already bankrupted Corizon,
  Armor Health and Wellpath. The legacy correctional EHRs are unfunded and, on the public record, many
  cannot even generate a superbill. The ambient-scribe leaders sell seats into Epic shops and have no
  claims path and no clinician workstation to sit on.
- **Expansion path.** ~3,000 county jails against roughly 9bn dollars of US correctional health spend
  → state prison systems and regional jail authorities, where contracts are eight and nine figures →
  the reentry care-coordination layer paid by Medicaid managed-care plans who inherit a very expensive
  member at the gate, which converts a procurement business into a payer-funded one → the general case
  of care for people the state is responsible for: juvenile facilities, state psychiatric hospitals,
  civil commitment, and health-related social needs under the same 1115 authority.
- **Scores.** Wedge 5 · Pattern 4 · Market 4 · Defensibility 4 · YC-fit 4 · Founder-fit 4 →
  **composite 4.2**
- **Biggest risk.** This is the most litigated setting in American healthcare and you are volunteering
  to hold the liability that bankrupted the last three operators — the honest record that is your wedge
  is also discoverable against you. Add slow county procurement, real reputational exposure with some
  investors and employees, and the fact that 19 approved waivers means 31 states where the revenue leg
  does not exist yet.

### 2. The machine company doctor — clear the Dutch sick-leave backlog the law says has to be cleared

**In plain English.** In the Netherlands, if an employee goes off sick the employer must keep paying
their wages for two full years, and must follow a strict legal procedure with fixed deadlines — the
first one at six weeks — to get them back to work. If the paperwork isn't good enough the government
can make the employer pay for a third year. The procedure has to be run by a specially registered
company doctor, and the country is running out of them: it needs at least 258 new ones a year and only
about 90 start, so the wait for an appointment has gone from one or two weeks to four or five, against
a six-week legal deadline. We become the occupational health service that employers hire, but with a
machine doing the interviewing, the assessment write-up and the deadline-tracking, and the scarce
doctor reviewing and signing — which Dutch rules already allow. Employers pay us the same fee they pay
today, and every day earlier we get someone back to work saves them 250 to 400 euros.

- **The proven pattern.** Tandem Health — 86.49M euro Series B on 2026-09-14, 10,000+ care
  organisations, the first AI scribe in Europe certified as an EU MDR Class IIa medical device,
  delivered through Accurx to 5,000+ NHS customers — proves that European clinical AI is a fundable
  category in its own right and that a model's clinical output can now carry regulated standing.
  The "own the licensed firm" half is proven by the YC S26 agent-native services cluster and Savvy
  Wealth.
- **Who buys the narrowed version.** The HR director or owner of a Dutch employer with 50 to 1,000
  staff — big enough to have continuous long-term absence, too small to run occupational health
  in-house. Trigger: an employee passing six weeks of absence, the statutory deadline for the problem
  analysis, at a moment when the incumbent cannot offer a doctor's appointment for another month; and
  annually, the arbo contract renewal date.
- **Why the leader can't follow.** A certified arbodienst must be accredited, must employ doctors on
  the Dutch BIG and NVAB registers, and carries statutory liability for a reintegration file judged by
  the UWV under Dutch employment law — standing no US health-AI company can acquire by localising. The
  incumbents (ArboNed/HumanCapitalCare, Zorg van de Zaak, 365) are staffing businesses: their revenue
  is the doctor's hour and their moat is the size of their doctor roster, so making one doctor do the
  work of ten deletes both at once. And Tandem's own product sells a clinical note into a care
  organisation; this output is a legal document in an employment dispute, bought by the employer.
- **Expansion path.** Dutch SME employers buying a mandatory, annually switchable arbo contract →
  up-market into the absence-heavy sectors where one employer's sick-pay bill is tens of millions →
  take the risk instead of the fee: Dutch sick-leave insurance is priced off payroll and sector because
  nobody can price *duration*, and whoever shortens duration and holds the outcome data can underwrite
  it → the rest of Europe, where occupational health is statutory almost everywhere — Belgium's
  external prevention services, Germany's mandatory works-doctor coverage and the six-week
  reintegration duty under section 167 SGB IX, France's mandatory prevention and occupational health
  services, Italy's competent physician.
- **Scores.** Wedge 4 · Pattern 4 · Market 4 · Defensibility 4 · YC-fit 4 · Founder-fit 4 →
  **composite 4.0**
- **Biggest risk.** The incumbents are already buying AI tools, so the race is whether an entrant can
  reach scale before one of them decides to cannibalise itself — and the beachhead is one mid-sized
  country, which means the venture case depends on the insurance leg and the European expansion, both
  of which are two moves away. There is also a founder-shaped risk: this needs a Dutch registered
  company doctor as a founding partner on day one, not as a hire.

## Method note for the next run

**One. Add a fifth question to the occupancy test: what did this buyer do before the moat opened?**
The EU Data Act family died four times for the same reason and the ordinary occupancy search did not
predict it — the occupants are not "startups niching this way", they are established businesses that
routed around the data monopoly with hardware years ago. A data-access regulation is only a why-now if
the workaround was *impossible*, not merely inconvenient. Asking "who has been serving this need with
duct tape, and how well does the duct tape work?" would have killed all four in one search instead of
four.

**Two. In Europe, stop working the calendar and start working the register.** Six European regulatory
triggers have now been tested across this book (CRA, Data Act, PPWR, AI Act Article 50, EUDR by
inspection, the Accessibility Act by inspection) and every one was occupied by an established
compliance-services industry or deferred. The thing that is *not* occupied is the licensed European
profession whose supply is falling against a statutory workload: the Dutch company doctor is one
(258 needed a year, 90 starting), and the same arithmetic is publicly documented for French
occupational medicine, for German notaries and for several national medical specialties. Next run
should start from a *capacity plan* — the official national workforce projections that say how many of
a licensed profession are needed versus how many are qualifying — rather than from a regulation's
application date. That is a list of markets where demand is legally fixed and supply is provably
falling, which is the cleanest why-now this book has found.

**Three. The US equivalent of that register is a distressed incumbent plus a new appropriation.**
Survivor 1 was found not from a funding round but from a single sentence in a policy document — that
correctional EHRs "were never designed to generate a superbill" — sitting next to three bankruptcies
and 19 approved waivers. That combination (the incumbent is insolvent, the buyer is switching now, and
a new funding stream just opened that the incumbent's systems structurally cannot collect) is worth
searching for directly. Concretely: next run should check the other section 1115 authorities on the
same pattern — health-related social needs, and the justice-involved benefit's sibling programmes —
and ask in each case whether the vendors serving the segment can produce a claim at all.
