**The survivor — ts-0133: evidence-constrained world models for the $443B tort system.** *(composite 4.5 — highest score in 140 ledger entries)*

When someone is badly hurt in a truck crash, the whole fight is over what actually happened — how fast, who braked, whether the injury even fits the impact. Today a law firm pays an engineer $25,000-$50,000 to hand-build a 3D cartoon of their preferred version, the other side pays a different engineer for the opposite cartoon, and it takes months. This is a machine that takes the *real* evidence — scene photos, dashcam footage, the truck's black box, the medical scans, the sworn testimony — and generates an accurate, physically realistic replay you can walk around inside, plus a record showing exactly which piece of evidence pinned down which fact, how certain each one is, and what other versions of events the evidence still allows. Lawyers and insurers pay because it's a fraction of the cost, arrives in days, and stands up when a judge asks whether the computer can be trusted.

**Why it's still open:** everyone who could build it is disqualified. The animation studios sell the artist-hours it deletes. The forensic-engineering firms (Exponent, Rimkus) sell the expert's personal credibility on the stand. The existing reconstruction software answers "simulate the scenario I typed in," not "find every scenario the evidence permits." The legal-AI leaders (EvenUp, Darrow) never touch the physical facts. And the world-model labs are selling horizontal 3D APIs.

**Why now, precisely:** World Labs' Marble world API went live two days ago; Genie 3 makes interactive physics-consistent worlds; inference is 90-97% cheaper than two years ago, so you can generate and score *thousands* of candidate reconstructions instead of one. Meanwhile the courts are writing the reliability rules for machine-generated evidence right now — proposed Federal Rule of Evidence 707, back in revision since June — which means the winner is whoever ships **defensibility**, not realism, and nobody owns that yet.

**Biggest risk:** admissibility. If judges reject AI-made reconstructions outright, the courtroom use dies — though 95% of cases settle, so the mediation and insurance-claims business survives either way.

**Killed:** AI clinical-simulation hours for nursing schools (UbiSim and Elsevier already there; the real blocker is a human-facilitator rule) · AI veterinary care on Colorado's new mid-level vet role (one state, effective 2027, and 95% of Colorado vets opposed it) · the compliance rail for the new federal school-choice money (the admin fee pool is too small to be a billion-dollar company) · killing the data pipeline with cheap inference (Fivetran, Databricks and Airbyte all shipped it) · reading every municipal bond disclosure (MuniBonds.AI and DPC Data got there) · AI for critical minerals in mine tailings (the Energy Department funded exactly this three days ago) · ambient billing-code capture for nursing homes (PointClickCare owns the data, and it's upcoding by another name).

**Two rules worth keeping:** if the structural half of an idea is on YC's current wish list, treat it as already saturated — use that list as an exclusion list, not an idea list. And check whether a platform owner already holds the data before spending a search.
