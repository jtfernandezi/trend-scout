# Origin of record — the underwritten country-of-origin determination

**Ledger id:** ts-0281 · **Date:** 2026-08-25 · **Composite:** 4.3
*Novelty 4 · Why-now 5 · Market 5 · Defensibility 4 · YC-fit 4 · Founder-fit 4*

## The one-line version

Reconstruct an imported part's true multi-tier bill of materials, issue a written country-of-origin
and regional-value-content determination, and indemnify the importer for back duties, interest and
penalties if CBP later denies the claim. Tax-liability insurance, but for origin — sold at the exact
moment the United States put rules of origin on an annual cycle and CBP lost the power to soften a
wrong answer.

## In plain English

When an American company brings a part into the country, it must declare which country the part
really comes from. That declaration decides whether it pays no tariff or a punishing one. Almost
nobody knows the true answer, because a part is made of parts made of parts, sourced from suppliers
on three continents who will not open their cost books. So companies guess, and their customs
broker files the guess, and everyone moves on.

The government can come back as much as five years later, reject the guess, and bill the back
tariffs plus interest plus a penalty — and from this September the discount it used to offer on that
penalty is being cut to a floor of half, with no discount at all for repeat offenders. A wrong guess
can now also cost a company its right to import at all.

This company does the real work of tracing the part all the way down, writes the answer down, and
then puts its own money behind it: if the government disagrees, we pay the bill. Importers pay a
premium for that the way homebuyers pay for title insurance — not for the search, for the promise.

## Why now — and why both halves are necessary

**The structural half.** Two dated triggers landed seven weeks apart.

- **2026-07-01 — the United States declined to extend the USMCA.** Mexico and Canada both supported
  the sixteen-year extension; the US "did not agree to renew the USMCA in its current form." Under
  Article 34.7.4 the agreement now runs on **annual joint reviews** until it is extended or expires
  on 2036-07-01. Preferences and rules of origin remain fully operative today, but a US push for
  country-specific automotive content thresholds is live into the September round and rules of
  origin are broadly expected to tighten. A determination made against this year's rules cannot be
  carried forward.
- **2026-06-03 — the "Strengthening Customs Enforcement" executive order.** Within 90 days
  (**2026-09-01**), CBP must revise every penalty mitigation standard to a floor of **not less than
  50% of the assessed penalty** and eliminate mitigation for repeat offenders. It further creates a
  **"good standing"** requirement that conditions import privileges themselves, sharply restricts
  foreign entities from serving as importer of record, and directs CBP to raise minimum bond
  coverage and require tangible domestic assets.

Underneath both: CBP verification and audit activity is already up sharply through 2025–26;
verification reaches back **five years** from entry; a denied claim reliquidates at MFN duty plus
stacked Section 232/301 duty plus interest plus penalties of up to **four times** the unpaid duty
for negligence under 19 U.S.C. 1592. A valid certificate of origin is a *declaration, not proof* —
CBP expects material-supplier invoices, bills of lading, production records and the cost analysis
behind the regional-value-content percentage. Mexico's January 2026 customs reform independently
added real-time AI-driven enforcement, 24-hour discrepancy reporting and broker joint liability, so
both sides of the border now enforce by machine. HS 2027 is next.

**The capability half.** Reconstructing regional value content to the sub-tier has always been an
evidence problem, not an arithmetic one. A part contains parts contain parts, from suppliers who
will not disclose cost build-ups, in formats that share no schema. Doing it properly costs more in
professional-services diligence than any premium could justify — which is exactly why nobody has
ever been willing to insure the answer. In 2026 that arithmetic inverted: twelve frontier models
from seven providers shipped in August alone, agentic runs now span dozens of turns and ~0.5B input
tokens, and frontier-class inference sits at $0.20/$1.20 per million tokens. An underwriter can now
afford to actually do the search on every part it insures, and to redo it continuously off the
customer's ERP and purchase-order feed as suppliers change.

**Neither half works alone.** Without the enforcement shift, being wrong is negotiable and nobody
buys certainty. Without the cost collapse, the diligence exceeds the premium and the product cannot
be priced.

## The insurable object

This is the crux, and it is where the conventional insurance market has reasoned itself out of a
market. The prevailing view is that tariff cost increases are not insurable: a duty bill is a known
cost imposed by policy rather than a fortuitous event, and the Government Action exclusion catches
anything ordered by a governmental authority. That is **correct about the duty rate** and
**precisely wrong about the determination**.

The insured event here is not "tariffs went up." It is "our determination that this part originated
in Mexico was wrong." That is fortuitous from the insured's perspective, diligence-sensitive from
the underwriter's, and structurally identical to two products that already work:

- **Title insurance** — the insurer does the search, insures its own search, and manages loss ratio
  by being good at searching.
- **Tax liability insurance** — insures a specific identified uncertain tax position. Euclid took a
  record **554 global submissions in 1Q26 against 418 in 1Q25**, with underwriting that reviews
  return positions and tax memoranda and increasingly incorporates predictive analytics on
  historical settlement patterns and authority enforcement trends. Demand is robust and growing.

There is no customs equivalent. That is the gap, and the reason it stayed open is that until this
year the search was unaffordable.

## Beachhead

The VP of trade compliance or CFO at a US mid-market manufacturer or importer with roughly
**$50M–$1B of annual entered value** in USMCA-claimed or Section 232/301-exposed goods — auto parts,
industrial machinery, electrical equipment, appliances.

Two trigger moments, both self-identifying:

1. **A CBP verification letter or Form 28/29 arrives** on a claim made up to five years ago. The
   exposure is already crystallising and the buyer will pay almost anything for a defensible file.
2. **Annual re-certification.** Origin has to be asserted for the coming year against rules that now
   change annually, and last year's file no longer discharges reasonable care.

Land at (1) — the pain is acute and the sales cycle is days — and convert to (2), which is the
recurring product.

## Path to $1B

1. **USMCA-claimed goods** across roughly $1.5T of US–Mexico–Canada trade. Start where the duty
   delta is largest and the RVC math is hardest: automotive and its sub-tiers, where a 75% regional
   value content threshold, a labour value content requirement and a 70% steel and aluminium rule
   already stack.
2. **All US preference and origin claims** — Section 232 and 301 substantial-transformation
   disputes across ~$3.3T of US imports, where transshipment through a third country is the single
   most enforced fact pattern.
3. **Non-US origin regimes** — EU preference claims, CBAM and EUDR origin evidence, and the HS 2027
   reclassification wave.
4. **Move up the stack and own the entry.** Once you carry the determination risk, becoming the
   importer of record is a capital decision rather than a product decision — and the June-3 EO's
   good-standing and tangible-domestic-asset thresholds are about to clear out the thinly
   capitalised IOR-service providers who currently occupy that role. (Logged separately as the
   ts-0271 kill precisely because it is an expansion, not a starting point.)

A premium of even 15–40 basis points on a modest share of entered value is a multi-billion-dollar
premium pool, and the loss ratio is a direct function of how good the model is — which compounds.

## Why incumbents structurally can't

- **Customs brokers** are agents, not principals. Their powers of attorney and terms push origin
  liability back to the importer, and they have no balance sheet to stand behind an answer. Guidance
  to importers is explicit: if something is wrong — even if it came from a supplier or a broker — it
  is the importer who pays.
- **Trade-compliance software** sells determinations and keeps the risk where it was. e2open,
  Descartes, Thomson Reuters ONESOURCE and Trade Insight AI's BOM-driven rules-of-origin engine all
  ship "audit-ready" output; YC has funded four companies in the same layer (MarkIt, Trava, Pax,
  Forge). Not one of them takes the loss.
- **Sureties** write customs bonds that guarantee CBP gets paid. The importer remains fully liable
  to the surety, so the bond transfers collection risk and never determination risk. Rising bond and
  collateral requirements under the June-3 EO make this *more* painful for importers, not less.
- **Conventional insurers** exclude government action and treat duty as a known cost. E&O and D&O
  respond to service-provider error and defence costs, not to the importer's own wrong call.
- **Big Four and trade law firms** bill hours for advice and will not indemnify a conclusion — and
  their cost structure makes per-part determination impossible at scale.

## Kill attempts survived

1. **"AI rules-of-origin engines already exist."** They do — Trade Insight AI markets exactly a
   BOM-driven, rule-versioned, audit-ready origin engine, and the ledger already killed ts-0025
   (AI customs broker / HTS classification) and ts-0028 (tariff engineering and duty optimisation)
   for occupancy. Survived because the determination layer being commoditised is the *premise*: the
   product here is the indemnity, and the determination is the underwriting.
2. **"YC has funded this four times."** MarkIt (AI agents for trade compliance), Trava (audits
   customs entries, automates classification), Pax (AI-native broker for tariff refunds), Forge
   (duty drawback). All software and services; none carries the loss. This cuts both ways and is
   reflected in the novelty score of 4 rather than 5 — the buyer is validated and the layer is
   crowded, so the pitch must be unmistakably the risk-taker.
3. **"Insurance already covers it, or can't."** Searched directly. Customs bonds guarantee CBP, not
   the importer. E&O and D&O reach service-provider error and investigation defence costs, not the
   importer's own determination. The specialised product — indemnifying an importer against its own
   classification or origin error — is not documented as existing, and the market's stated reasoning
   for its absence conflates the duty (uninsurable) with the determination (insurable).

## What would kill it

**Feature risk.** MarkIt or Trava partners with an MGA and staples a warranty onto a determination
they already produce. The defence is speed to a real book and loss experience: the second entrant
has to price against your loss history without having one.

**Correlated tail risk.** This is the serious one. A title insurer's losses are independent; these
are not. A single rules change, a single CBP enforcement campaign against a fact pattern, or a
single adverse ruling can hit the entire book at once, five years deep. The first two years get
priced with no loss history. Mitigations exist — per-part aggregate limits, exclusions for
retroactive rule changes, reinsurance from day one, deliberate diversification across duty regimes
and part families — but they have to be designed into the first policy, not retrofitted.

**Moral hazard and drift.** An insured changes a sub-tier supplier and the determination silently
goes stale. This is why the product must be a continuous subscription wired into the customer's ERP
and PO feed rather than a one-time opinion — which is also the lock-in and the data moat.

**Regulatory whiplash.** The February 2026 Supreme Court ruling against across-the-board IEEPA
tariffs shows the duty landscape can move fast in both directions. A sharp broad de-escalation
shrinks the duty delta and with it the willingness to pay. The annual-review structure makes that
less likely — uncertainty is now the regime, not the exception — but it is the honest bear case.

## First 90 days

- Take three real importers' actual USMCA files and rebuild their RVC to the sub-tier by hand plus
  model. Measure two things: how many of their current determinations are wrong, and what it cost
  you to find out. Those two numbers are the entire business case.
- Get a fronting carrier or MGA structure and a reinsurance conversation started in parallel — this
  is a licensing and capital build, not just a product build, and it is the long pole.
- Land the first customers on the verification-letter trigger, where the exposure is already real,
  and sell the recurring product on the way out.
- Instrument everything: every determination, every piece of supporting evidence, every CBP outcome.
  The loss file is the moat.
