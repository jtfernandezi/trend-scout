# Capability credit for the defence sub-tier

**Ledger id:** ts-0282 · **Date:** 2026-08-25 · **Composite:** 4.5
*Novelty 4 · Why-now 5 · Market 5 · Defensibility 4 · YC-fit 5 · Founder-fit 4*

---

## The one-line version

Lend $2M–$20M of pre-delivery working capital to Tier-2 and Tier-3 defence manufacturers,
underwritten not on financial statements or collateral but on a frontier model's judgement —
formed from the technical data package, the shop's machine base, and its real first-article and
non-conformance history — of whether that specific shop can build that specific part on that
specific schedule.

## In plain English

America and Europe have ordered far more weapons than their factories can make. The choke point is
not the big prime contractors; it is the thousands of small, often family-owned machine shops,
forges and electronics manufacturers that make the parts inside the parts. Those shops are paid on
delivery, so taking an order three times their current size means buying metal, tooling, a machine
and a second shift with money they do not have. Banks will not lend it to them, partly because of
defence-related compliance and reputational policies, and mostly because no loan officer can look
at an engineering drawing and tell you whether a forty-person shop can really make twelve thousand
of that part on time.

This company can. It reads the drawings and specifications, maps them against the machines the shop
actually owns and its documented record of parts passing inspection, forms a view on whether the
shop can deliver, and lends against the order. The shop ramps, the prime gets its parts, and the
lender gets interest — plus, over time, the only dataset in existence of what shops like this one
actually deliver versus what they promised.

## Why now — and why both halves are necessary

**The structural half.** The demand shock is dated, quantified and on two continents.

- Oliver Wyman surveyed 160-plus US defence supply-chain companies in May 2026 and puts the backlog
  on a path to roughly **$900B**, describing sub-tier suppliers as the structurally exposed segment
  with "the least financial, operational, and digital room to absorb the surge."
- In Europe, defence orders roughly **doubled between 2025 and early 2026 while industrial
  production edged up only marginally**. Rheinmetall's group backlog is close to six times the
  midpoint of 2026 sales guidance. Named constraints include skilled labour, fragmented
  certification, and **limited advance payments for sub-tier suppliers**.
- Banks decline defence SMEs on compliance and reputational grounds, especially for classified or
  unpredictable contracts. Up to **half of lower-tier suppliers do not seek debt finance at all**.
  Payment terms are on delivery or milestone — the wrong side of the cash-flow problem.
- Public capital is arriving *above* the gap. The FY26 NDAA expanded the Defense Industrial Base
  Fund and ~$20B of credit subsidy supports industrial-base lending; DoD's Office of Strategic
  Capital writes **$10M–$150M** tickets on government timelines. Nothing addresses a $4M loan to a
  shop in Ohio that needs it in six weeks.
- Business Executives for National Security published the thesis under its own headline: *Equity
  Capital for the Defense Industrial Base: Why Private Investors Can't Underwrite What They Can't
  See.*

**The capability half.** The underwriting question is an engineering judgement, not a credit score.
Answering "can this shop deliver this order" means reading a technical data package — drawings with
geometric dimensioning and tolerancing, material and process specifications, special-process
callouts — and mapping it against the shop's spindle availability and machine base, its historical
first-pass yield on comparable features, its non-conformance and corrective-action record, its raw
material dependency, and the prime's delivery schedule. That is multimodal engineering reasoning
over unstructured artefacts. Until 2026, no lender could put a manufacturing engineer on a $5M loan
and still make money. With twelve frontier models from seven providers shipping in August alone,
long-horizon agentic runs spanning ~0.5B input tokens, and frontier-class inference at $0.20/$1.20
per million tokens, that judgement now costs less per deal than a site visit.

**Neither half works alone.** Without the demand shock there is no borrower and no urgency. Without
the capability collapse the diligence costs more than the loan earns — which is exactly why this
lending does not exist today.

## Beachhead

The owner of a US Tier-2/Tier-3 defence machine shop, forging, castings house or electronics
manufacturer with 20–200 employees, at the moment a prime offers or awards a surge or multi-year
order worth two to five times current revenue.

Why this buyer is winnable now: the pain is acute, dated and self-identifying (there is a signed or
offered order and a known funding gap), the alternatives have all just said no, and the decision
sits with one person who owns the company. There is no compliance programme to sell into and no
committee. It is a sales call.

Start with one part family and one prime's supplier list — 155mm-adjacent machining, or solid
rocket motor components, or connectors and harnesses — so the model's promised-versus-delivered
dataset densifies in one place before it generalises.

## Path to $1B

1. **US defence sub-tier working capital.** Pre-delivery finance against awarded orders. The
   reachable need is in the tens of billions and recurring, and it renews with every order.
2. **Equipment and capex finance for the same shops.** Once you can judge what a shop can build,
   you can judge what a marginal machine adds to what it can build — a better underwrite than the
   resale-value logic equipment lenders use today.
3. **Europe.** SAFE/EDIP money is flowing into the same bottleneck, and the sub-tier advance-payment
   gap is explicitly named as a constraint. The underwriting object is identical.
4. **All of reindustrialisation's Tier-2/3.** Semiconductor tooling, grid and transformer
   components, rail, nuclear. Sequoia is steering roughly $10B at exactly this thesis.
5. **Co-lending and guarantees.** OSC's $20B of credit subsidy and the expanded Defense Industrial
   Base Fund are a rail, not a rival — they cannot originate at this ticket size or speed. Being the
   origination and underwriting engine in front of public capital is how the balance sheet scales
   without becoming the constraint.

A specialty lender scales with capital; the durable asset is the underwriting edge and the
origination flow. The moat is the labelled dataset of what shops like this one actually delivered.

## Why incumbents structurally can't

- **Banks** — explicit compliance and reputational exclusions on defence SMEs, and no mechanism to
  price execution risk on a print they cannot read.
- **Factoring / purchase-order finance** — prices the receivable, which exists only after delivery.
  The shop's entire problem is upstream of the receivable.
- **Equipment lenders** — underwrite the machine's resale value, not the order. Wrong asset, wrong
  amount, wrong time.
- **JPMorgan-style defence supply-chain inventory finance, ECA and SBLC structures** — built for
  primes and Tier-1s with treasury departments.
- **Office of Strategic Capital** — $10M minimum, government process, policy-priority screening.
- **Private credit** — declines for BENS's stated reason: it cannot see inside a small shop, and
  the diligence to see inside costs more than the loan earns. That cost is the thing that just
  changed.

## Kill attempts survived

1. **"Someone already lends to defence suppliers."** They lend to primes and Tier-1s (JPMorgan
   defence supply-chain inventory finance, ECA/SBLC stacks) or against machines (specialty equipment
   lenders, who weight asset coverage and backlog). Nothing found underwrites execution capability
   at the sub-tier.
2. **"Adjacent startups already cover this."** The funded neighbours — Silkline AI (RFQ-to-PO cycle
   compression in high-mix low-volume manufacturing) and Vendra (technical requirements to qualified
   US suppliers) — orchestrate sourcing. They do not extend credit and do not take the risk. Hadrian
   and Xometry take the *order*, which is the ts-0280 kill, not this.
3. **"The government will do it."** OSC's credit programme is live and lends $10M–$150M with
   project selection, underwriting and monitoring phases. It structurally cannot serve a $4M loan on
   a six-week clock, and the FY26 NDAA credit subsidy makes it a co-lending partner rather than a
   competitor.

## What would kill it

**The AI-native gate, honestly.** This ledger killed ts-0041 (GPU-collateralised lending) because
the underwriting was structured telemetry that needed no frontier model, leaving a moat made of
credit expertise and capital. That failure mode is live here and must be disproven empirically, not
argued: if the first twenty loans get made on backlog quality, prime relationships and gut feel with
the model producing a memo nobody reads, this is an ordinary specialty lender with a good story. The
test is whether model-derived capability findings change the credit decision — approve deals a bank
would decline, and decline deals that look fine on paper — often enough to show up in the loss rate.

**Secondary risks.** Correlated default if a programme is cancelled or a prime re-competes an award
mid-ramp; ITAR, CMMC and classified-adjacency requirements on data handling that are a real
engineering build before the first loan; and adverse selection, since the shops that come to you
first are the ones everyone else declined — which is survivable only if the capability read is
genuinely better than the credit read.

## First 90 days

- Get five to ten technical data packages from real awarded sub-tier orders and build the capability
  read offline. Backtest against known outcomes: which of these shops actually delivered on time?
- Sign one prime as a referral channel. Primes have a direct interest in their sub-tier being
  fundable and know exactly which suppliers are capacity-constrained.
- Write the first three loans off a small facility or fund vehicle, deliberately small, to generate
  labelled promised-versus-delivered data.
- Open the OSC and Defense Industrial Base Fund conversation early — a guarantee or co-lending
  structure changes the cost of capital more than any rate negotiation will.
